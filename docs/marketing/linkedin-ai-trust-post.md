There's a good reason a lot of people don't trust AI. And in healthcare, that distrust is healthy.

I'm a pharmacist. I've spent years being the last check before a medicine reaches someone — so the idea of handing judgement to a black box makes me uneasy too. When I started building a tool that uses AI, I had to be honest with myself about that.

Here's how I've landed on dealing with it:

1. Don't feed it what it doesn't need. The safest data is the data you never collect. So it holds no patient details at all — the AI never sees a name, an NHI, anything that identifies a person. You can't leak what you don't have.

2. AI suggests, the human decides. Always. It can point at a pattern or suggest a fix, but it never makes the call — the pharmacist does. It's an assistant, not an authority.

3. Make it optional. If you don't trust it, turn it off — and the tool still works. Trust shouldn't be a condition of using something.

4. Be able to explain, in plain words, exactly what it does with your data. If you can't explain it simply, that's usually a sign you shouldn't be doing it.

None of this is clever. It's just treating AI the way we'd treat a new staff member — useful, worth having, but checked, supervised, and never left to sign things off on its own.

The trust problem with AI isn't going away. But I don't think the answer is to trust it more. I think it's to build it so you don't have to.

How do you decide when to trust an AI tool — or not?
